# CourtIQ Performance Engine

## Purpose
The performance engine explains why physical training matters for tennis and ties it directly to on-court outcomes. It is not a generic fitness system; it is a club-level tennis conditioning guide.

## Core principles
- Tennis is a stop-start sport with repeated sprint, recovery, and directional change demands.
- The highest payoff comes from improving court movement, recovery between points, and durability across a match.
- Strength work should support joint stability, racket control, and explosive movement—not bodybuilding size.
- Conditioning should improve repeatability, not just one-rep max power.

## VO2 max explained for tennis players
- VO2 max measures how much oxygen your body can use during hard exercise.
- For tennis, a higher VO2 max means you recover faster between points and maintain sharper decision-making in long rallies.
- Tennis players do not need elite endurance; they need a tennis-specific aerobic base combined with short anaerobic bursts.
- Training focus: improve the body’s ability to recover quickly after a sprint or hard rally.

## Strength vs hypertrophy rules
- Strength training = build force production and joint stability.
- Hypertrophy training = build muscle size and work capacity.
- In tennis, prioritize strength by using moderate loads and controlled movement quality.
- Use hypertrophy-style sets only when the goal is injury resilience and muscular balance, not size.
- Keep squat, hinge, and rotational exercises simple and functional.

## Conditioning logic
- Tennis movement is reactive and repeated; use interval work that mirrors point structure.
- Prefer short energetic efforts followed by active recovery.
- Include change-of-direction drills, shuttle runs, and low-impact recovery sequences.
- Avoid long steady-state cardio as the main tool; use it only to build aerobic base.

## 15 training sessions
1. Tennis movement primer: split-step, first-step, and agility ladder.
2. Court recovery session: interval jog / walk with directional changes.
3. Lower-body stability session: single-leg balance and hinge control.
4. Reactive speed session: short shuttle runs and partner calls.
5. Shoulder control session: banded rotations and scapular work.
6. Core control session: anti-rotation and loaded carry drills.
7. Deceleration session: reverse lunges and braking steps.
8. Explosive short-ball session: squat jumps and lateral bounds.
9. On-court endurance session: tennis-style intervals with jog-back recovery.
10. Movement economy session: footwork pattern repeatability.
11. Rotational power session: medicine ball throws and cable chops.
12. Mobility recovery session: hip, shoulder, and thoracic flow.
13. Stability + strength session: moderate-load compound lifts.
14. Tempo control session: varied-speed court steps and shuttle repeats.
15. Warm-up and prep session: dynamic activation for tennis practice.

## Practical guidance
- Each session should feel relevant to tennis movement and not like a gym workout.
- Use simple exercises with clear carryover to on-court court speed and stability.
- Avoid programming excessive volume; keep each session short and purposeful.
- Every training block should answer the question: "How will this make my next match feel easier?"
